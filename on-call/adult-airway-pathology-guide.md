
# Adult Airway Pathology Guide

A consult framework for the adult patient presenting with stridor, acute voice change, or a suspected airway lesion. For the quick-reference version of the stridor consult, see [Consults](consults.html#airway-patients-with-stridor). This guide is adult-focused — for neonatal/pediatric airway complaints, see [Consults — Pediatric/Neonatal Stridor](consults.html#pediatricneonatal-stridor) and [Pediatric Vocal Fold Paralysis](../specialties/pediatric-otolaryngology/common-conditions.html).

## First: Is the Airway Safe Right Now?

Before you take a history, triage:

* **Vitals:** O2 sat, RR, HR, work of breathing, ability to lie flat/tolerate secretions
* **Visual exam:** retractions (supraclavicular, intercostal, suprasternal), tripoding, accessory muscle use, cyanosis, drooling
* **Voice:** can they speak in full sentences? A muffled/"hot potato" voice suggests supraglottic pathology
* If anything looks unstable, **call the attending before you finish your history** and get airway equipment (bag-mask, nasal trumpet, Heliox, scope, surgical airway tray) to the bedside

Only once the patient is confirmed stable do you move into a full history/differential/workup.

## History

* **Onset:** sudden (foreign body, angioedema, anaphylaxis, acute vocal fold paralysis after a procedure, traumatic) vs. gradual (neoplasm, subglottic stenosis, bilateral vocal fold immobility, slow-growing mass)
* **Rate of progression:** hours (infection, angioedema, hematoma) vs. weeks-to-months (tumor, stenosis, progressive neurologic disease)
* **Character of the noise/voice:**
  * Stridor vs. wheeze (patient- or family-reported "wheeze" is frequently actually stridor — don't take the label at face value)
  * Breathy/weak voice vs. strained/strangled voice vs. muffled voice vs. complete aphonia
  * Positional component — worse supine (bilateral vocal fold immobility, tracheomalacia, a mass with a ball-valve effect) vs. worse with exertion (fixed obstruction, cardiopulmonary disease)
* **Preceding events / exposures:**
  * Recent intubation, difficult or traumatic intubation, prolonged intubation
  * Recent surgery — thyroid/parathyroid, anterior cervical spine, cardiothoracic (CABG, aortic arch repair, lung apex resection), carotid, skull base
  * Head/neck radiation history
  * Trauma (blunt or penetrating neck injury)
  * Known malignancy, heavy smoking/alcohol history
  * Recent URI, sick contacts
  * New medications (ACE inhibitors, new antibiotics/NSAIDs for angioedema/anaphylaxis)
  * Foreign body/choking episode
* **Associated symptoms:**
  * Dysphagia (solids/liquids/pills), odynophagia, "stuck" sensation, choking or coughing with intake, recurrent aspiration pneumonia — suggests glottic insufficiency or a mass effect
  * Hemoptysis — raises concern for malignancy or an eroding process
  * Weight loss, night sweats — malignancy until proven otherwise
  * GERD/LPR symptoms — heartburn, throat clearing, globus
  * Neurologic symptoms — tremor, gait change, other cranial neuropathies (consider Parkinsonism/MSA/ALS or a skull base/jugular foramen process)
* **Past medical/surgical history:** autoimmune disease (granulomatosis with polyangiitis, sarcoidosis, relapsing polychondritis — all can cause subglottic/tracheal disease), prior airway surgery or instrumentation, prior radiation, known vocal fold pathology

## Differential Diagnosis

Organize by **anatomic level** — this also maps directly onto the phase of stridor you hear on exam.

| Phase of stridor | Level | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Inspiratory** | Supraglottic/glottic | Collapsible, extrathoracic segment |
| **Biphasic** | Glottic/subglottic | Fixed, extrathoracic segment |
| **Expiratory** | Trachea/bronchi | Intrathoracic segment |

### Supraglottic
* Epiglottitis/supraglottitis (adults get this too — don't anchor on "it's a pediatric disease")
* Deep neck space infection tracking to the supraglottis (Ludwig's angina, retropharyngeal/parapharyngeal abscess)
* Angioedema (ACE-inhibitor, hereditary, allergic/anaphylactic)
* Supraglottic mass/malignancy

### Glottic
* Unilateral or bilateral vocal fold immobility (surgical, malignant, idiopathic, neurologic)
* Laryngeal/glottic edema (post-instrumentation, inhalation injury, angioedema)
* Laryngospasm
* Glottic web, granuloma, papillomatosis
* Glottic malignancy
* Arytenoid dislocation (post-intubation — can mimic vocal fold paralysis on exam)

### Subglottic/Tracheal
* Idiopathic subglottic stenosis
* Iatrogenic (post-intubation/post-tracheostomy) subglottic or tracheal stenosis
* Granulomatosis with polyangiitis, relapsing polychondritis, sarcoidosis
* Tracheomalacia
* Tracheal mass (primary or invading from thyroid/esophagus)
* Tracheoesophageal puncture/prosthesis complications in laryngectomy patients (see [Consults — TEP Displacement](consults.html))

### Systemic / Other
* Anaphylaxis
* Foreign body (fish bone, food bolus, aspirated object)
* Traumatic airway injury (blunt or penetrating neck trauma, inhalation/burn injury)
* Post-surgical neck hematoma (classic after thyroid/parathyroid surgery — can compress the airway rapidly)

**Quick framing questions that narrow this list fast:**
* Infectious vs. inflammatory (angioedema) vs. neoplastic vs. traumatic vs. neurologic vs. iatrogenic?
* Is there a temporal relationship to a recent procedure/surgery/intubation?
* Is the voice affected (glottic/supraglottic process) or is the voice normal despite a tight airway (think bilateral paramedian vocal folds)?

## Workup

### Bedside / First-Line
* Continuous pulse ox, RR, work of breathing
* **Flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy** — this is the single most important test in almost every adult airway consult. It directly visualizes the supraglottis, glottis (position, mobility, mucosa), and proximal subglottis, and immediately tells you whether you're dealing with a mechanical/structural process, edema, a mass, or immobility.
  * Don't force a scope through a tight, unvisualizable airway — if you can't pass it safely, stop and get the attending; this is an OR conversation, not a bedside one.
* Voice quality assessment alongside the scope (breathy/weak = glottic insufficiency; strained/strangled = possible bilateral paramedian folds or spasmodic component)

### Imaging
* **CT neck/chest with contrast, skull base to carina** — the workhorse study when there's a vocal fold immobility, a suspected mass, or no obvious cause on history; it traces the full course of the vagus/recurrent laryngeal nerve and evaluates the trachea and mediastinum
* **CXR** — faster first-line option if a CT can't be obtained urgently, or as an initial screen for a mediastinal/apical lung process; doesn't adequately image the skull base
* **CTA** if there's a vascular concern (trauma, suspected great vessel involvement, cavernous sinus thrombosis in the setting of orbital/deep neck infection)
* **MRI** if a skull base or neurologic process is suspected

### Labs
* CBC, CMP as indicated by the clinical picture
* **TSH** if vocal fold immobility and thyroid status unknown
* **C4, C1-esterase inhibitor level** if angioedema without a clear ACE-inhibitor/allergic trigger (suspect hereditary angioedema)
* **ANCA (c-ANCA/PR3)** if subglottic/tracheal stenosis with no intubation history — think granulomatosis with polyangiitis
* Cultures as indicated for infectious causes

### Functional / Adjunct Studies
* **Pulmonary function tests with flow-volume loop** — useful in the stable, outpatient setting to characterize and quantify a fixed vs. variable extrathoracic obstruction (e.g., subglottic stenosis, bilateral vocal fold immobility) and to follow response to treatment
* **Swallow evaluation (bedside, or formal MBS/FEES)** if there's dysphagia or aspiration concern, especially with glottic insufficiency from vocal fold immobility
* **Laryngeal EMG** for vocal fold immobility of uncertain etiology or duration — most informative **3-6 months** from onset, to distinguish recoverable neuropraxia from fixed denervation and guide timing of any permanent intervention
* **Microlaryngoscopy/bronchoscopy** when the diagnosis remains unclear after the above, when tissue is needed, or when the procedure itself is therapeutic (dilation, biopsy, foreign body removal)

## Management by Acuity

1. **Airway widely patent, mild findings, slow progression** → observation with continuous O2 monitoring; treat the underlying cause
2. **Airway patent but moderate compromise (e.g., early angioedema, post-extubation edema)** → ICU-level observation, racemic epinephrine, IV steroids, re-scope before changing level of care
3. **Unable to fully visualize the airway distal to an obstruction** → OR for awake fiberoptic intubation, with a surgical airway already set up
4. **Unable to see any airway at all, or rapidly progressive** → OR for awake tracheostomy

### Surgical Airway Pathway
1. **Call the attending immediately**
2. Nasal trumpet; Heliox if increased work of breathing (bridges, doesn't treat)
3. Bag-mask (may be difficult with supraglottic pathology) or LMA as a temporizing measure
4. If stable enough, fiberoptic exam; if intubation is needed, do it **in the OR** with a surgical airway already prepped

## Two Entities Worth Calling Out Specifically

### Bilateral Vocal Fold Immobility — Treat as an Emergency Until Proven Otherwise
* **The voice can be deceptively normal** — bilateral paramedian folds can preserve a near-normal voice while the glottic airway is critically narrowed. Don't be reassured by a good voice alone.
* Classic cause: bilateral RLN injury from thyroid/parathyroid surgery — always on the differential in a post-thyroidectomy patient with any respiratory symptom, along with a compressive neck hematoma
* Other causes: prolonged intubation, neurologic disease (Parkinsonism/MSA, ALS), idiopathic
* If there's any respiratory symptom: scope immediately, evaluate the neck for hematoma, and call the attending — this may need the OR (awake fiberoptic intubation or tracheostomy) rather than floor observation

### Unilateral Vocal Fold Immobility — Usually Not Emergent, Still Needs a Workup
* Voice is affected (breathy, weak, diplophonia); airway is usually fine; aspiration can be significant if the glottic gap is large
* Get the CT skull-base-to-chest, TSH, and a swallow eval if there's dysphagia; many idiopathic/post-surgical cases recover over 6-12 months, so avoid rushing to permanent intervention before an EMG/observation window has passed
* Temporary injection augmentation is reasonable early for significant dysphonia/aspiration without precluding later recovery; permanent medialization is for confirmed non-recovering paralysis

## Quick Red Flags

* Stridor + inability to lie flat or handle secretions → surgical airway pathway, not the floor
* New hoarseness after thyroid/parathyroid surgery + any respiratory symptom → rule out bilateral vocal fold immobility and neck hematoma before anything else
* A normal-sounding voice does not rule out a dangerous bilateral vocal fold process — scope before you reassure anyone
* Hemoptysis, weight loss, or heavy smoking history with a new airway complaint → treat as malignant until proven otherwise
* Dysphagia/aspiration with vocal fold immobility → involve SLP and get a swallow evaluation early
