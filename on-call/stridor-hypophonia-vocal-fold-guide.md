
# Stridor, Hypophonia & Vocal Fold Immobility Guide

This is a deep dive for consults centered on the airway/voice complex. For the quick-reference version, see [Consults](consults.html#airway-patients-with-stridor). For neonatal/NICU stridor, see [Consults](consults.html#pediatricneonatal-stridor) and the [Pediatric Vocal Fold Paralysis](../specialties/pediatric-otolaryngology/common-conditions.html) workup.

## First Question on Any of These Consults: Is the Airway Safe Right Now?

Before taking a history, quickly triage:

* **Vitals:** O2 sat, RR, HR, work of breathing, ability to lie flat/tolerate secretions
* **Visual exam:** retractions (supraclavicular, intercostal, suprasternal), tripoding, accessory muscle use, cyanosis, drooling
* **Voice:** can they speak in full sentences? A muffled/hot potato voice suggests supraglottic pathology (epiglottitis, deep space infection, supraglottic mass)
* If anything looks unstable, **get the attending on the phone before you finish your history** and have airway equipment (bag-mask, nasal trumpet, Heliox, scope) at the bedside

## Part 1: The Stridor Consult

### History

* **Onset:** sudden (foreign body, angioedema, anaphylaxis, vocal fold paralysis after a procedure) vs. gradual (neoplasm, subglottic stenosis, bilateral VF immobility)
* **Rate of progression:** hours (infection, angioedema) vs. weeks-months (tumor, stenosis)
* **Preceding events:** recent intubation/surgery, trauma, radiation, known malignancy, recent URI, choking episode
* **Positional/exertional component:** worse supine (bilateral VF immobility, tracheomalacia) vs. worse with exertion
* Differential bucket: **infectious vs. inflammatory (angioedema/anaphylaxis) vs. foreign body vs. neoplastic vs. traumatic vs. neurologic (bilateral VF immobility)**

### Exam — Localize by the Phase of Stridor

| Phase | Localization |
|---|---|
| **Inspiratory** | Supraglottic or glottic (collapsible, extrathoracic) |
| **Biphasic** | Glottic or subglottic (fixed, extrathoracic) |
| **Expiratory** | Tracheal (intrathoracic) or bronchial |

* Perform a **fiberoptic exam** on essentially everyone who can tolerate it — this is the single most useful thing you can do. Look at the base of tongue, epiglottis, arytenoids/interarytenoid mucosa, and vocal fold mobility/position.
* If you cannot pass a scope (total obstruction, patient can't tolerate it) — don't push it. Get the attending and consider going straight to the OR.

### Management Algorithm

1. **Airway widely patent, mild edema/findings, slow progression** → observation on the floor with continuous O2 monitoring, treat the underlying cause (steroids, antibiotics, antihistamines as indicated)
2. **Airway patent but moderate edema (e.g., early angioedema, post-extubation edema)** → ICU observation, racemic epinephrine, IV steroids, re-scope before any escalation/de-escalation of level of care
3. **Unable to fully visualize the airway distal to the obstruction** → OR for awake fiberoptic intubation, with a **surgical airway setup** available
4. **Unable to see any airway at all / rapidly progressive** → OR for **awake tracheostomy**

### Surgical Airway Pathway — Checklist

1. **Call the attending immediately** — do not manage this alone
2. Nasal trumpet; **Heliox** if increased work of breathing (lowers turbulent flow resistance, buys time — it is a bridge, not a treatment)
3. Bag-mask (may be difficult if supraglottic pathology) or LMA as a temporizing measure
4. If the patient remains stable enough, perform a fiberoptic exam; if intubation is required, **do it in the OR** with ENT present and a surgical airway already set up/prepped
5. Have a real plan before you walk into the room: who is intubating, what's the backup, who is doing the trach if intubation fails

### Adult Etiology Considerations

* **Post-thyroid/parathyroid/anterior neck surgery** → think bilateral RLN injury or hematoma (see Part 3)
* **Post-extubation stridor** → laryngeal/subglottic edema is most common; consider arytenoid dislocation or vocal fold paralysis if hoarseness accompanies it
* **Angioedema** → see the Angioedema section in [Consults](consults.html) for the full workup and hereditary angioedema treatment ladder
* **Known malignancy or heavy smoking history** → scope for a mass; don't assume "just edema"

## Part 2: The Acute Hypophonia / Voice Change Consult

"Hypophonia" and acute voice change consults are frequently inpatient, post-operative, or post-procedural. The key branch point is: **is this a benign, self-limited process (edema, mild laryngitis) or is it a vocal fold immobility that needs a workup?** You cannot answer this without scoping the patient.

### History

* **Timing:** immediately post-extubation/post-op (think mechanical — edema, arytenoid dislocation, RLN injury) vs. days-to-weeks (think neuropraxia resolving, or a slower process like malignancy)
* **What procedure/surgery preceded it, if any:**
  * Thyroidectomy/parathyroidectomy — direct RLN or SLN injury
  * Anterior cervical spine surgery — RLN traction injury, usually from retraction
  * Cardiothoracic surgery (CABG, aortic arch repair, PDA ligation, lung apex resection) — **left RLN** is especially vulnerable given its course under the aortic arch
  * Prolonged intubation / difficult intubation — arytenoid dislocation, granuloma, edema, or a stretch injury to the RLN
  * Skull base or carotid surgery — can affect the vagus itself (combined RLN + SLN deficit)
* **Associated symptoms:** dysphagia (especially to liquids — a sign of glottic insufficiency and aspiration risk), choking/coughing with intake, recurrent pneumonia, aspiration, breathy or weak voice, vocal fatigue, or frank stridor/dyspnea (raises concern for a bilateral process — see Part 3)
* **Other causes to keep on the differential:** viral laryngitis (self-limited, days), LPR/GERD, malignancy (lung apex/Pancoast tumor, esophageal, thyroid, skull base/jugular foramen lesions), neurologic disease (stroke, Parkinsonism, ALS, myasthenia gravis), idiopathic (often presumed viral neuritis)

### Exam

* **Flexible laryngoscopy is mandatory** — you cannot diagnose vocal fold immobility, distinguish it from edema/hematoma, or assess airway/aspiration risk without directly visualizing the folds
* Assess: vocal fold position (median, paramedian, intermediate, lateral, cadaveric), mobility (fully immobile vs. hypomobile/paretic), glottic closure pattern and gap size, mucosal edema/hematoma, arytenoid position/rotation (arytenoid dislocation vs. paralysis can look similar — palpation/EMG can distinguish them if it's unclear)
* Listen to the voice quality: breathy (glottic insufficiency), strained/strangled (consider bilateral paramedian folds or spasmodic component), diplophonia (asymmetric vibration)

### When It's Just Edema

* Mild post-extubation or post-instrumentation edema with normal, symmetric vocal fold mobility → reassurance, voice rest, consider a short PPI course if reflux is suspected, re-scope if not improving in 1-2 weeks
* Don't reflexively start steroids or antibiotics for isolated hoarseness without a laryngoscopic finding to justify it

### When to Move to a Vocal Fold Immobility Workup

Any of the following should trigger the workup in Part 3:

* Frank immobility (not just edema/erythema) on scope
* Voice change persisting beyond ~2 weeks without improvement
* Voice change with dysphagia/aspiration symptoms
* Voice change after any of the surgeries listed above

## Part 3: Vocal Fold Immobility / Paralysis Workup

**The single most important branch point: unilateral vs. bilateral.** They present almost oppositely and are managed completely differently.

| | Unilateral | Bilateral |
|---|---|---|
| **Voice** | Breathy, weak, diplophonia | Often surprisingly normal/near-normal |
| **Airway** | Usually fine | **Often the emergency** — folds sit paramedian and obstruct |
| **Aspiration** | Can be significant (glottic gap) | Less common (folds are closer together) |
| **Urgency** | Outpatient workup in most cases | May be an airway emergency requiring immediate action |

### Unilateral Vocal Fold Immobility

* **Etiologies:**
  * Surgical/iatrogenic: thyroid/parathyroid, carotid endarterectomy, anterior cervical spine, cardiothoracic (especially left-sided given the RLN loops under the aortic arch), skull base surgery, prolonged/traumatic intubation
  * Malignant: lung apex (Pancoast tumor), esophageal, thyroid, mediastinal, skull base/jugular foramen (with other lower cranial neuropathies — consider a combined vagal/SLN+RLN picture)
  * Idiopathic/presumed viral neuritis — a diagnosis of exclusion
  * Neurologic: brainstem or skull base process affecting the vagus
* **Workup:**
  * **CT with contrast from the skull base to the carina/upper chest** — this traces the entire course of the vagus/RLN and is the single best imaging study when the cause isn't already obvious (e.g., a known recent thyroidectomy)
  * A **CXR** is a reasonable first-line/faster study if a CT can't be obtained emergently, but doesn't image the skull base or mediastinum in detail
  * TSH/thyroid function if not already known
  * **Swallow evaluation (bedside, or formal MBS/FEES)** if there is any dysphagia or aspiration concern
  * **Laryngeal EMG** — most useful **3-6 months** out from onset (or once the diagnosis is otherwise unclear) to distinguish neuropraxia (expect recovery) from a fixed/denervated fold (unlikely to recover), which guides timing of permanent intervention
* **Management:**
  * Idiopathic and post-surgical cases: many recover spontaneously within 6-12 months — don't rush to permanent intervention
  * **Temporary injection augmentation** (e.g., with a resorbable filler lasting weeks-months) is reasonable early if there is significant dysphonia or aspiration, since it doesn't preclude later recovery
  * **Permanent medialization** (thyroplasty, arytenoid adduction) is reserved for confirmed, non-recovering paralysis, generally after the EMG/observation window
  * SLP referral for voice therapy and swallow strategies in essentially all symptomatic patients

### Bilateral Vocal Fold Immobility — Treat as an Airway Emergency Until Proven Otherwise

* **Why the voice can be deceptively normal:** in bilateral immobility the folds often sit paramedian and close to the midline — this can preserve a reasonably normal-sounding voice while the glottic airway is critically narrowed. **Don't be reassured by a good voice in this setting.**
* **Etiologies:**
  * Bilateral RLN injury from thyroid/parathyroid surgery (the classic cause — always have this on the differential in a post-thyroidectomy patient with any respiratory symptoms)
  * Prolonged intubation
  * Neurologic disease: Parkinsonism/multiple system atrophy, ALS, other brainstem/central processes
  * Idiopathic
* **Immediate evaluation:**
  * Assess for stridor, retractions, tachypnea, inability to lie flat — these mean the airway is tight
  * Scope to confirm bilateral immobility and characterize the position (paramedian folds obstructing the posterior glottic chink is the classic finding)
* **Management:**
  * **Call the attending immediately** — this is not a "wait and scope in the morning" consult if there is any respiratory symptom
  * If there is airway compromise: this goes to the **OR** — options include awake fiberoptic intubation with a definitive airway plan, or **tracheostomy** if intubation isn't safe/feasible. Definitive procedures for the vocal folds themselves (cordotomy, arytenoidectomy, suture lateralization) are typically staged later once the acute picture is stabilized, not performed emergently
  * If there is a component of edema/hematoma (e.g., immediately post-thyroidectomy with a neck hematoma) — **evaluate the neck first**; a hematoma causing airway compromise needs the wound opened at the bedside if truly emergent, don't wait for imaging
  * Even if stable at the moment, admit for airway monitoring and serial exams; a paramedian bilateral immobility can decompensate with any additional edema (e.g., from positioning, fluids, or a subsequent minor illness)

## Quick Red Flags

* Stridor + inability to lie flat or handle secretions → OR/surgical airway pathway, not the floor
* New hoarseness after thyroid/parathyroid surgery + any respiratory symptom → rule out bilateral VF immobility and neck hematoma before anything else
* "Normal-sounding" voice does not rule out a dangerous bilateral vocal fold process — scope before you reassure anyone
* Dysphagia/aspiration with unilateral immobility → get SLP and a swallow evaluation involved early, don't wait for the definitive ENT procedure
